The commander of the entire French army was about to do the most reckless thing a general can do.
The attack on the bridge at Arcole has stalled. The soldiers won't move, and Napoleon has just reached the front.
He has read about this moment since he was a boy. When Julius Caesar's army was breaking, Caesar grabbed a shield and walked to the front line himself. And the army held.
Six months earlier, at the bridge of Lodi, Napoleon learned the same thing: men will run into fire for a leader who stands in it with them.
You're Napoleon, commander of the whole army. If you die, everything collapses. Would you still climb onto that dike?
So he grabs a flag.
He climbs onto the dike, in full view of the Austrian guns, fifty paces from the bridge. He raises the flag and shouts at his men to follow their general. His officers and a handful of soldiers rush up beside him.
Then the Austrians open fire.
Officers start falling around him.
And right beside him stands an old friend, a man who knew Napoleon long before anyone knew his name.
