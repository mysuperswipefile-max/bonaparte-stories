Napoleon threw fourteen thousand men at Wellington's line. Within an hour, it turned into a disaster.
Waterloo, early afternoon. Prussians are coming toward Napoleon's flank, and Wellington still holds his ridge.
He chooses to attack.
He orders Grouchy to march toward the Prussians. The order will not reach him until after eight in the evening.
Eighty French cannons are already pounding the ridge. But the soaked ground swallows many of the shots.
Then General d'Erlon's corps marches forward: about fourteen thousand men in deep columns, against roughly six thousand defenders.
The British general Picton orders a counterattack, and is shot dead.
For a moment, Napoleon is winning.
Then two brigades of British heavy cavalry come thundering down the slope. They smash the French columns. The Scots Greys capture a French eagle.
You're Napoleon. Enemy cavalry is tearing through your infantry. Do you pull back, or strike back?
He strikes back. The British horsemen gallop on too far, and French lancers and cuirassiers cut them down. The Union Brigade's commander, Ponsonby, is killed.
D'Erlon's corps loses about three thousand men.
Then, around four o'clock, Marshal Ney sees British troops moving to the rear. He thinks Wellington is retreating.
