Napoleon lost Waterloo. But who really lost it for him?
After nine hours of fighting, Napoleon's army is fleeing into the night, chased by Prussian cavalry.
Around nine, near an inn called La Belle Alliance, Wellington and Blücher meet in the dark as victors.
According to a friend's diary, Wellington called it the nearest-run thing you ever saw in your life.
Miles away, Grouchy has spent the afternoon fighting a Prussian rearguard at Wavre. General Gérard urged him to march to the sound of the guns. Grouchy kept to his orders.
So who lost Waterloo?
The rain, which delayed the attack until late morning? Clausewitz believed an earlier attack would probably have won before the Prussians came.
Grouchy, who never came?
Ney, who sent the cavalry with no infantry?
Or Napoleon himself, who split his army and gambled his last Guard?
Within days, he gives up the throne. He surrenders to a British ship and is sent to Saint Helena, a rock in the middle of the Atlantic. He dies there in 1821.
Ney is shot by a firing squad in Paris that December.
