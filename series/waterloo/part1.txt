February 1815. Napoleon, the Corsican outsider who made himself emperor, has been beaten and exiled to the island of Elba.
In late February, he slips away with about a thousand men and lands in the south of France.
At a village called Laffrey, the king's soldiers block the road.
Napoleon walks up, opens his coat, and calls out: "If any of you will shoot his Emperor, here I am."
Nobody fires. The soldiers join him.
Even Marshal Ney, who had promised to bring him back in an iron cage, changes sides.
On the twentieth of March, Napoleon is back in Paris. The king has fled.
But Europe has already declared him an outlaw. Britain, Prussia, Austria and Russia raise armies against him.
Two are already in Belgium: the Duke of Wellington's British-led army, and Marshal Blücher's Prussians.
Apart, each can be beaten. Together, they could crush him.
So you're Napoleon. Do you wait for them in France, or strike first?
Napoleon has always believed that speed wins wars.
Before dawn on the fifteenth of June, his army crosses into Belgium.
